Zero-shot time-series foundation models (TSFMs) are increasingly deployed on the premise that
they outperform trained baselines without any local training, and public leaderboards report
this advantage against baselines fit on whatever history each benchmark series provides. We
show that on the widely used ETT benchmark, part of this advantage is a measurement artifact
of baseline training budget. Holding tuning, lookback, and evaluation windows fixed at strong
settings and sweeping only the number of training points K, the gap between a gradient-trained
DLinear baseline and zero-shot Chronos is positive and significant at a short budget (K=600;
+0.06 to +0.41 MASE across the four ETT series, all confidence intervals excluding zero) and
shrinks to statistical insignificance at a finite budget of order one to ten thousand points.
The shrinkage is not clean: on ETTh1 the gap re-widens at K=5000, so "parity" means the
difference is no longer detectable, not that the models are equivalent. The effect reproduces
across three models spanning two pretraining lineages, survives a tuning-controlled
decomposition on three of four series, and is bounded: on two hourly non-ETT domains the gap
stays significant past 10,000 points under a linear baseline (shrinking to +0.09 to +0.14
MASE), and with only two usable domains we cannot fit a law predicting where the artifact
applies. Two decisions follow: benchmark authors reporting a zero-shot advantage should
disclose the training budget given to each baseline, and practitioners holding more history
than the closing budget should sweep the baseline's budget before concluding that zero-shot is
better; on ETT, a short evaluation split alone inflates the reported gap.
